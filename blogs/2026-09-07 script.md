# overview
Motivation: Confidence is ahead of evidence.
Observation: Wrong reasons -> wrong solutions
Claim: Code is abundant, strategy is scarce.
Nuance: AI speed depends on the task and the system.
Opportunity: Speed is a currency, not the outcome.
Choice 1: spin the wheel faster or expand it?
Choice 2: Cost story or a growth story?
Close: What strategy serves your vision?


# slide 1 - title + opening
Hello! Let's talk about what is happening in the field of software development.

1. By a quick show of hands, how many of you have an AI strategy... that your board is satisfied with?
2. Keep your hands up if you are confident that this strategy is returning value on investment.
3. And keep your hands up if you have hard evidence that supports that confidence: metrics, customer outcomes, financial results

I ask because we have often seen a gap form between confidence and evidence, where many enterprise AI programmes get stuck.

## slide 2-7 Observation:
As an industry, software development is undergoing its most significant shift since the dawn of the internet.
Yet, years in, there is little tangible evidence that businesses are by and large enjoying a positive bottom-line impact from this transformation.
why is that? i have a theory and i’d be curious if you did too?

---
first of all, i observe that too often, AI adoption is doing the right thing for the wrong reasons, and hence, poorly.

---
I contend that AI adoption is the right thing for a software developement teams to work on now. I am guessing I don't need to convince this room of that. 
the capability and the promise of ai is staggering. sitting this one out is hardly an option.
and it matters how it's approached. 

---
many organisations are being driven to AI by the wrong motivations:
- FOMO: a worry that competitors are pulling ahead.
- pressure from above, your CEO forwards a tech-press article. an investor sees a slick demo. a vendor slide promises 10x productivity.
- a.i. is smart, ergo it is also complicated. rather than trying to understand the tech (hard work), refer to the wisdom of crowds and follow the concensus.
- a.i. adoption is one thing for a nimble startup and another for an established, regulated enterprise. so avoid comparing apples and oranges.
- there is shadow i.t., because developers are seeing all this, they are ambitious, curious, maybe impatient. 
these forces and tendendancies are  understandable. they are familiar. they are shared across the industry. 

but note that 
organisations driven by fear     ask: "can we say we did it?"
organisations driven by strategy ask: "did this move the needle?"
those are different programmes and they produce different results.

---
tools-first thinking generates activity without impact:

- a.i. code generators produce reams of code that clog delivery pipelines because the rest of the SDLC can't keep up.
- each team inventing its own a.i. adoption leads to fragmented and unauditable implementations. 
- a.i. can accelerate technical debt faster than it solves valuable problems.
- technology keeps evolving faster than organisations can adopt it, adopting technology for the sake of technology leaves you chasing your tail.

---
and after all the effort 
- the return on investment is a ghost. often hinted at, believed in by some, but invisible whenever you look for it.

note that 
- The ambition is real.
- The investment is real.
- The urgency is real. 
But these do not add up to a strategy.


# slide 8-10 abundance and scarcity
my second observation is this.:
> Code is abundant, strategy is scarce.

today, a.i. generates more code than humans do. and the bots are producing it faster and faster. code is not scarce. code is not hard to come by. want some code? no problem.

the scarce resource is:
- Choosing the right problem
- Explicitly scoping the problems and determining what's out of scope 
- Optimal trade-offs across customers, teams and time
- Turning technical capacity into an outcome that matters


---
combining the two leads me to believe that strategy is key. 
here, by strategy, i mean it in the practical sense: the layer between vision and plan.

a strategy brings focus. focus means is a thousand “no”s for every “yes”. it says: this is what we want, this is what we do. this is what we don't do and will stop doing, and this is how we will know whether the choice is achieving what we intended.

a vision tells us why we exist. a plan tells us what to do next. strategy makes the choices that connect the two.

example choices:
- easy: what should we build?
- harder: what should we not build?
- even harder: what do we need to stop building?

A bad strategy lists tools and solutions. a list of 'yes's
A good strategy describes the trade-offs. 'no's and 'yes's

# slide 11-16 speed is real—but speed is not the outcome
AI coding tools CAN deliver real speed and efficiency gains. The capability is real. But the outcome is conditional.

The take away lesson from published research is nuanced is not that “AI is fast” or “AI is slow”. It is: the effect depends on the task, the system, the people and the way the work is organized. so to get the speed gains that ai makes possible, requires careful engineering of the context to maximise constrained autonomy, narrowing the tasks passed on the llms. 

You always get more code is moving through the system, but that does not automatically mean faster outputs and better outcomes to customers.

This is why I want to separate out three tiers of success metrics:
1. **Activity:** how much usage your AI tooling gets. Adoption rates, token counts, how many times your engineers run out and need to await the next refresh window.
2. **Output:** lines of code, amount of documentation, count of commits or pull requests that ai tools produce.
3. **Outcome:** what improved for customers, for the business bottom line, or for the people doing the work.

Activity is easy to count/track. but it is an effort measure, not what we are aiming for.
Output is trickier, as not all of it can be correctly attributed to ai. 
Outcome metrics take genuine effort, not just to measure but mainly to correctly attribute. Outcome is what the strategy is supposed to create.

---

so let's imagine we have engineered the conditions in which generated code can be trusted, tailored the scaffolding around ai tools, and that we are properly measuring outcomes not activity, furthermore we have adopted a.i. across the sdlc proccess pipeline and we see that ai clearly increases speed and efficiency. and we are spinning the wheel faster than ever. that's great! right? i mean it's just the same wheel. but faster. 

that's not the only option, though. i think of that speed boost like a currency. it can buy you a further reach. 

problems that for the longest time have been too involved to take on, can now become possible to tackle:
- Legacy code modernisation, 
- Technical debt payback before software assets becomes a legacy liability
- Documentation automation. 
  - Better documentation coverage, 
  - Documentation kept up to date, 
- Comprehensive testing,
- More test case scenarios, 
- Previously unviable product projects turn into strategic investment areas.
- Experimentation with novel new features, 
  - Research into expanded use cases, 
  - New markets

---
- just think of whatever it is in your sdlc that you would like to improve, and have wanted to improve for a while, but never gets prioritised. 
  - this single important, but never urgent task. it's too much work, takes too much time. 
- what if adopting ai gave you more time, and reduced the amount of effort required? 
  - what opportunities for improvements could that bring into reach? 
  - what new value can you unlock for your business? 
  - what new markets and customer use cases could you serve?
- we don't need to limit the impact of ai to spinning the same wheel faster. 
  - we can expand the wheel.

---
- a focus on speed and efficiency makes ai adoption a cost savings exercise. 
  - cost savings are great: for one thing, they are measureable. 
  - you have the baseline costs, and if those sink towards zero, your cfo will love you for it. it's great.
-what's even better than saving money? 
  - maybe making money? 
  - by expanding what you can deliver, 
  - by increasing output quality, and appeal, 
  - ai adoption could instead be a growth story.

of course, it is not either/or. every business must in reality balance both. you still have the day-to-day (business as usual) optimisations. 
it's a balance of productivity and possibility. my point here, is to not lose sight of the possibilities. to think beyond efficiency to what it can buy you. 

---
in this age of a.i. thinking strategically about these opportunities, and balancing acts is more impactful that thinking through detailed code puzzles.

i maintain that this trades labour for meaningful work. work that connects with the purpose and vision of the organisation. 
- quality of life upgrade for developers

---
i also want to tackle one particular aspect of the cost story. and that is the -in my opinion- wrong headed. 
that's the talk of ai taking the jobs of developers. this is a description of a.i. adoption as means for swapping out carbon based programmers for silicon based agents. 

i don't find the argument as convinceing as so many do. 
imagine two businesses in the same sector. both have the same staff costs and revenue, so share the same thin margin. 

company a adopts a.i. with a a zero-sum mindset. the more work AI does, the less you need employees to do. they cut down their staff by half. costs go down, without productivity budging at all. margins go up. success. happy times. but that's getting stuck in a local optimum.

their competitor, b, instead retained their staff, now empowered with armies of ai agents. by connecting the adoption with strategic goals, they were able to extend their reach, growing their market presence, conquering new markets, customising user experience, inventing new features and solutions.
broader experimentation, higher quality, better validation, better documented, better managed, more robust. 

Replace people and shrink the organisation. Amplify your people with strategic mindset and you grow ambitions. 
Both are success stories. One is a cost story. the other a revenue story. Which company does your vision document describe?

AI as a replacement for talent: yields brittle, untrustworthy systems, and a demoralised team.
AI as an engine for empowerment yields a company of superheros capable of building something extraordinary.

Which company does your vision describe?

# closing.
i will end where i began: “Code is worthless. Strategy is Everything.”

My title is deliberately provocative and overstated with the intention to spur thoughts and conversations. Code is obviously not worthless. we still need code to achieve all the goals of our strategy. software still has to run. It still has to be secure, reliable, understandable and useful.

if only i had been a bit more clever when cecilia asked me to commit to a title, i might have said 

> code is lead. (a heavy, dull, plentiful, liability. a health risk.)
> strategy is gold (the key that opens all opportunities, grants you passage anywhere you want to go. a wealth risk.)

so with that adjustment out of the way: it's time for questions and answers. i will start:

What is the one thing in your SDLC pipeline that you would love to fix, but it is just out of reach?
What does your company strategy tell you not to do? What are your thousand "no"s?
What does your strategy require you to stop doing?
What does your vision drive you to do with the speed increases that a.i. adoption can bring about? more cycles? more quality? more breadth? more depth? more spread?
What growth becomes impossible if half your dev team is replaced by AI? 
What competitive ground might you cede to the competitor who keeps and empowered their engineers instead?
